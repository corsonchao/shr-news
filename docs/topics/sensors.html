Groß Schönebeck geothermal research site in Germany celebrates 25th anniversary

A research facility in Germany called Groß Schönebeck has been studying geothermal energy (heat from deep underground rocks) for 25 years, using a converted old natural gas well. Scientists there have developed important techniques for finding and accessing geothermal resources, including methods to map underground rock structures and measure their properties. Recently, they're testing new technology that can extract heat directly from deep boreholes without pumping water to the surface, which could make geothermal energy more practical and scalable worldwide.

Geothermal cell connectivity between Cerro Pabellón Dome and Apacheta-Aguilucho Volcanic complex (northern Chile) as confirmed by noble gas monitoring

Researchers used noble gases (rare gases naturally present in the Earth) to track connections between two geothermal systems in northern Chile: Cerro Pabellón Dome and Apacheta-Aguilucho Volcanic complex. This monitoring technique helps scientists understand how hot fluids and gases move underground between different geothermal reservoirs (underground formations containing hot water and steam), which is important for developing geothermal energy resources in the region.

▲ Single-well geothermal heating pilot project installed in Munich, Germany

A new pilot project in Munich is testing a single-well geothermal heating system that could bring geothermal heat pump technology (a system that extracts heat from underground water to warm buildings) to urban areas where space is limited. Unlike traditional systems that need two separate wells, this innovative design uses just one well with special internal piping to extract and reinject groundwater, taking advantage of natural groundwater flow to make the system more affordable. Tests will begin in 2026 at a children's center, with the system operational by 2027.

▲ Hephae Energy closes $17.8m Series A funding round to support superhot geothermal drilling

Hephae Energy, a company developing advanced drilling equipment for superhot rock geothermal projects, has raised $17.8 million in funding to commercialize its specialized drilling tools. The company's Pandora210® system can operate in temperatures up to 210°C (much hotter than the current industry standard of 175°C), and they are developing next-generation equipment capable of handling even hotter temperatures above 300°C. This funding will help the company scale up production and bring these heat-resistant drilling technologies to market for geothermal energy companies in the US and internationally.
