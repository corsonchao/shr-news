▲ Icelandic Orkuveitan sees up to 50 MW potential for IDDP-3

An Icelandic energy company is planning to drill a deep well called IDDP-3 to test whether superhot geothermal wells (boreholes that tap into extremely hot rock deep underground) can produce much more electricity than traditional geothermal wells. Currently, conventional geothermal wells reach temperatures of 250-350°C and produce 2-7 megawatts of power, but this new project targets depths of 4-5 kilometers where temperatures exceed 400°C, with the goal of producing 20-50 megawatts per well. The company plans a four-stage testing program starting in late 2026 to prove the concept works and to develop materials and equipment that can safely handle these extreme underground conditions.

▲ Can Heat-as-a-Service unlock geothermal scale?

Heat-as-a-Service is a financing model where an investor owns and operates geothermal heating infrastructure while customers pay for heat through long-term contracts, removing the need for customers to fund expensive upfront costs. This approach can make geothermal more accessible to businesses and buildings, though larger utilities may prefer to maintain ownership and control of their heating systems. Combining multiple smaller projects into larger investment portfolios and offering both heating and cooling services can make geothermal financing more attractive to investors.

Berlin heat planning maps geothermal at urban scale

Berlin is using detailed mapping to identify where geothermal systems (which use boreholes to extract heat from underground rock and soil) could heat and cool entire neighborhoods and buildings, rather than just individual homes. The city analyzed about 26,000 city blocks to figure out which areas have enough geological potential, available space, and economic viability for these systems. Carbfix achieves 99.6% capture of CO2 and H2S from geothermal power plant in Iceland

A company called Carbfix has successfully captured 99.6% of harmful gases (carbon dioxide and hydrogen sulfide) released from a geothermal power plant in Iceland. The captured gases are dissolved in water and injected deep into rock layers underground, where they permanently turn into minerals—a process that normally takes nature thousands of years but Carbfix speeds up to just two years. This achievement shows that geothermal energy can be made much cleaner by removing its pollutants before they escape into the atmosphere.
