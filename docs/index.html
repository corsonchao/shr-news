▲ Start of drilling imminent for geothermal heating project in Gräfelfing, Germany

A German town called Gräfelfing is preparing to drill two deep wells into underground rock layers about 2,900 meters below the surface to access hot water for heating. The hot water, which will be around 100°C (212°F), will be used to heat homes and buildings through a district heating network (a system that distributes heat from a central source to multiple buildings), and the cooled water will be pumped back underground. If successful, the project could provide up to 25 megawatts of thermal energy (a measure of heat power) and begin supplying heat by 2029.

▲ Endurance Energy demonstrates subsea geothermal power in Pacific Ocean

A company called Endurance Energy successfully tested an underwater geothermal power system at a volcanic seamount 300 miles off Oregon's coast. The system drilled into the ocean floor and used hot water from deep-sea hydrothermal vents (natural hot springs) to generate electricity, producing 16.5 kilowatts of power, though only briefly. While the test had technical issues, it proves that underwater geothermal power generation is possible, and the company plans to develop commercial systems and deploy them near Tonga by 2027.

▲ Eavor withdraws drilling rigs from the Geretsried geothermal project site in Germany

Eavor Technologies is temporarily removing its drilling equipment from its commercial geothermal project in Geretsried, Germany to reduce costs while it works on improving the project's business arrangements. The project, which uses Eavor's closed-loop geothermal technology (a system that circulates fluid through underground rock to extract heat without requiring traditional water reservoirs), started producing electricity but fell significantly short of expectations, generating only 0.5 to 2.0 megawatts instead of the planned output. The company plans to resume drilling for a second loop once it secures better commercial agreements with local heating partners.

▲ News roundup – The geothermal projects eligible for $99M funding from the US DOE

The U.S. Department of Energy has identified 21 geothermal projects eligible to receive approximately $99 million in funding for testing enhanced geothermal systems (EGS)—a technology that artificially creates fractures in hot rock underground to improve heat extraction—and for exploration drilling. Multiple companies and research institutions are proposing projects across the western U.S. that range from drilling deep wells to test superhot rock potential to enhancing existing geothermal fields, with the goal of demonstrating that these technologies can reliably produce commercial-scale power. All selected projects are currently in negotiation with the DOE and have not yet received final funding commitments.

▲ Tender – Consultancy for exploration of Hot Springs geothermal project, Oman

Oman's government is seeking consulting help to explore a geothermal project at Hot Springs, where heat from within the earth could potentially be used for energy. The consultants will perform geological surveys, assess how much heat energy is available underground using 3D computer modeling, and prepare plans for temperature-gradient drilling (drilling to measure heat at different depths). This is part of Oman's step-by-step plan to develop its geothermal resources, following earlier work that analyzed data from over 7,000 existing wells to find promising locations.

▲ Mitsui Fudosan starts demonstration of single-well closed-loop geothermal in Japan

A Japanese real estate company is testing a new type of geothermal system that uses a double-walled pipe inside a single well to extract heat from hot rock deep underground without pumping out water or steam. This closed-loop approach aims to generate power while reducing environmental impact on both the surface and subsurface. The company plans to verify whether this technology can work economically before building a full commercial plant.
