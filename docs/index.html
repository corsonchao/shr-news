ThinkGeoEnergy · 2026-10-23 high

▲ STEAM completes geochemical and gravity surveys at Karthala geothermal field, Comoros

A company called STEAM has completed detailed underground surveys at Karthala, a volcanic geothermal field in Comoros, to prepare for drilling deep exploration wells. The surveys measured gravity (how density changes underground), collected gas samples from volcanic vents, and combined this data with previous studies to create an updated model showing where hot rocks and fluids are located. Results indicate reservoir temperatures around 275–315 °C and confirm this is a viable high-temperature geothermal system (one that naturally produces very hot fluid), with the northern area identified as the best location for the first deep exploration well.

▲ Identifying geothermal prospectivity zones in the Fergana Valley through lineament density and land surface temperature anomalies

Researchers developed a method to identify promising areas for geothermal energy development in the Fergana Valley by analyzing two types of data: lineament density (patterns of cracks and fractures in rock that help heat flow) and land surface temperature anomalies (areas where the ground is unusually hot). This technique helps geothermal explorers find the best locations to develop new projects without drilling unnecessarily.

▲ First contract awarded for deep geothermal exploration drilling in Berlin, Germany

Berlin has awarded a contract to explore deep geothermal energy, which involves tapping into hot rock layers beneath the ground to generate heat. A company will drill about 2,000 meters deep at a power plant site to collect rock samples and test the water-bearing layers to determine if geothermal energy could reliably heat the city. This exploration is the first practical step toward using this abundant, emissions-free heat source to help Berlin transition to climate-neutral energy.

Geothermal Canada · 2026-10-21 high

▲ Eavor withdraws drilling rigs from the Geretsried geothermal project site in Germany

Eavor Technologies is temporarily removing its drilling equipment from its geothermal project in Geretsried, Germany to reduce costs while the company works on improving the project's business arrangements. The site began producing electricity in 2025 using Eavor's closed-loop geothermal technology (a system where fluid circulates in sealed underground pipes to extract heat), but it only generated between 0.5 to 2.0 megawatts of power with minimal electricity reaching the grid. The company plans to continue developing the project and eventually drill a second loop once it secures better agreements with local heating customers.

Geothermal research in Thurgau, Switzerland to continue with 3D seismic survey

Researchers in Switzerland are studying deep geothermal resources (heat stored in hot rock layers far underground) and have found three different water-bearing rock layers at various depths with different temperatures. They plan to conduct a 3D seismic survey (using sound waves to map underground structures) across 430 square kilometers in 2027-2028 to better understand where the hottest and most usable geothermal resources are located, which will help communities and businesses develop their own geothermal projects.

Temporal changes in seismic response of the Tres Vírgenes geothermal field (Mexico) from HVSR: Reservoir-scale variability and depth sensitivity

Researchers studied the Tres Vírgenes geothermal field in Mexico using a seismic measurement technique called HVSR (horizontal-to-vertical spectral ratio) to understand how the underground rock layers change over time. By analyzing how seismic waves move through the subsurface (the layers of rock and soil beneath the surface), they discovered that different depths and locations within the geothermal reservoir show varying responses, which could help improve how geothermal energy is extracted from hot rock formations.

Drilling down on how Canadians feel about geothermal energy

A recent survey of about 2,500 people in Alberta and British Columbia found that roughly half of Canadians have little to no familiarity with geothermal energy (heat extracted from within the Earth), making it far less well-known than other major energy sources. The research suggests geothermal is a promising clean energy option that most Canadians simply haven't learned about yet.
